Seven problems, seven fixes

Version 2 described an economy that worked on paper and would have failed in practice. Here is exactly what was wrong and what replaces it. Everything else in this document follows from these seven decisions.

Problem 1 — the squeeze

Rewards shrank every season while upgrading and defending land got more expensive. Eventually nobody could afford anything.

Fix — every price is indexed to earnings

Nothing is priced in tokens. Everything is priced in Days of Walking — what a typical player earns in a day. If rewards halve, prices halve, so the grind never gets longer. Each upgrade also raises that land's income by about the factor its cost rose, holding payback near 38 days at every level.

Problem 2 — only long distances paid

A 750-metre walk was worth almost nothing, so the product quietly belonged to athletes.

Fix — the first kilometre is the valuable one

Your first kilometre counts fully; the tenth barely counts. A marathon earns 4.7× a 750-metre walk, not 56×. Short walkers become first-class players and distance farming stops working.

Problem 3 — free players had nowhere to spend

Their locked balance grew for ever and meant nothing. A number that only goes up is not an economy.

Fix — a real everyday currency with a full Vault

Stride replaces "Locked MOVE": earned by everyone, spent on everything, held in a Vault with a limit. Fill the Vault and you are visibly wasting walks — so you spend, and money circulates.

Problem 4 — new players could not get land

Land cost tokens. New players had no tokens. The ladder had no bottom rung.

Fix — land is claimed by showing up

Every player gets a Home Plot free. Further plots need Stride plus proof you go there. No money, token or wallet needed to own ground. Tradeable Deeds are an upgrade you earn later or buy for about $9.

Seven problems, seven fixes

The remaining three are about money leaving the system: what players are asked to give up, what they are allowed to sell, and whether the sums added up in the first place.

Problem 5 — nobody wants to burn what they earned

"The top 100 landowners burn 10% of their yield" is a punishment for succeeding. Players would have hated it.

Fix — Ground Rent, waived by visiting

A small, predictable rent on land you hold — like council tax. Visit often enough and it is waived entirely. Active players pay nothing; absentee speculators pay every season.

Problem 6 — sell pressure at 70% crypto users

If most players sell most of what they earn, the market must absorb more than it can.

Fix — Quick Claim at 60%, or Full Claim at 100%

Take 60% today or wait 30 days for everything. Nothing is confiscated, and the impatient fund the patient. At the worst case our sinks absorb 3.8× the selling.

Problem 7 — emissions did not add up

Pools shrinking 20% a season total 45M tokens ever, against 600M reserved for players — thirteen times apart.

Fix — decay to a floor, not to zero

The pool falls fast for five years, then settles at a sustaining level. The reserve now funds rewards for 56 years, and the numbers agree.

Part 1 · Earning

The idea that fixes the squeeze

This is the most important page in the document. It is the answer to "as rewards shrink and costs rise, won't players eventually be unable to afford anything?"

We never price anything in tokens. We price everything in Days of Walking.

1 Day of Walking = what a typical active player earns in one day

Measured continuously as the median across everyone playing. Today that is about 150 Stride. If earnings fall by half, a Day of Walking is 75 Stride — and every price in the game halves with it.

Why this changes everything

In version 2, a plot cost a fixed number of tokens. Rewards fell every season, so that fixed price quietly became further and further out of reach — a grind that gets longer every year until it becomes impossible. Players notice this within weeks and leave.

With Days of Walking, the price and the wage move together. A plot costs about thirteen days of walking in year one, and it still costs about thirteen days of walking in year ten. The economy can shrink, halve, or triple, and the player's experience of it never changes.

The same plot, the same player, two pricing rules. One economy dies of its own arithmetic; the other does not.

Where the idea comes from

This is not invented. It is index-linking — the mechanism countries use to stop inflation quietly destroying pensions and wages. Pensions in the UK, social security in the US, and wage agreements across Europe are all tied to an index rather than to a fixed sum, precisely so that a number agreed today still means the same thing in twenty years. We are doing the same thing to game prices.

How to explain it in one sentence

"Prices in MovenRun are set in days of walking, not in tokens — so however the token economy moves, a plot always costs about a fortnight of getting out for a walk."

Part 1 · Earning

Two currencies, two jobs

Version 2 had one token wearing two hats: locked for free players, liquid for landowners. It confused everyone and it still let balances pile up for ever. We split them properly.

The pattern every successful game economy uses: a soft currency you earn constantly and spend constantly, and a hard currency that is genuinely rare.

The Vault — why a limit is a feature

Stride is held in a Vault that starts at 5,000 and can be upgraded to 50,000 and beyond. Once it is full, further earnings are lost.

That sounds harsh until you see what it does. A balance that only grows is not money — it is a scoreboard, and scoreboards do not create an economy. The Vault limit means every player has a standing reason to spend: on the next plot, the next level, the next contest. It converts a growing number into circulating money. Upgrading the Vault is itself one of the most common purchases in the game, which is exactly how it works in Clash of Clans and every game economy built since.

Nobody is ever forced to spend. But everybody can see their Vault filling, and that is enough.

The rule that prevents the classic exploit

Stride can never be converted into $MOVE at a rate. If it could, free accounts would become token farms overnight — the exact failure that killed the last generation. The only route from free play to $MOVE is earning a deed and qualifying for the season pool, which takes real time in a real place and cannot be automated at scale.

Part 2 · Land

The land ladder

Three kinds of land, three ways to get it. The bottom rung is free, and every rung above it can be reached by walking rather than by paying.

The ladder has a free bottom rung and no step that requires money. Paying only ever buys you time.

Why this solves the new-player problem

The old design had a chicken and egg: you needed tokens to buy land, and you needed land to earn tokens. New players had no way in, and free players had no way up. The ladder breaks it in three places:

  • The Home Plot is free and immediate. Nobody starts with nothing, and nobody can be evicted from where they live.
  • Plots cost Stride, which everybody earns. Thirteen days of ordinary walking, and land is yours. No wallet is involved at any point.
  • Deeds are a conversion, not a purchase. You cannot buy a deed on land you have not held and used. That single rule stops speculators from buying up a city before players arrive — which is what ruined most virtual-land projects.

Part 2 · Land

What land costs, and why

Version 2 said a plot costs "500 × the square root of weekly movers". Nobody outside a spreadsheet can act on that. Here is the same idea in words anyone can use.

Busy places cost more. But not proportionally more — or the city centre would be unreachable for everyone but the rich.

The rule in one line: a place that is four times busier costs twice as much. Nine times busier costs three times as much. Sixteen times busier costs four times as much. Prices rise, but they rise gently, so a popular high street stays within reach of an ordinary player who is willing to save for it.

The price list players actually see

Rather than a formula, the app shows a plot's band. Every price is in Days of Walking, so it never drifts.

The most valuable ground in a city costs about eight times what the quietest costs — even though it may be a hundred times busier. That gap is deliberate: it keeps the map contestable rather than letting the centre belong permanently to whoever arrived first.

The part that is not about money at all

Presence, not price

Stride alone will not get you a plot. You must also have visited it on five separate days within the last fortnight. This is the rule that makes MovenRun's land different from every other virtual land project: you cannot own somewhere you do not go. It cannot be bought remotely, it cannot be accumulated by a fund, and it cannot be farmed by a script that never leaves a building.

And a rule that protects the map from being carved up

Holding many plots in the same district raises the Ground Rent on all of them — a progressive rate, exactly like income tax bands. Your first three plots in a district are taxed at the standard rate; the fourth to eighth pay half as much again; beyond that the rate doubles. A player who genuinely lives and moves across a district can comfortably hold a handful. A speculator trying to corner it finds the cost rising faster than the reward.

Part 2 · Land

Upkeep, and the rent that replaces the burn

Land has to cost something to hold, or the map fills up with absentee owners and newcomers find nothing left. But the cost must never feel like a punishment for playing well.

Condition — and why walking is the repair

Every plot has a Condition from 0 to 100%. It falls slowly — about 2% a day — and a plot below 60% earns less and defends worse.

StepN made you pay tokens to repair your shoes, and players resented it because it felt like a meter running. We do it the other way round:

Visiting restores it — free

Walk through your plot and Condition jumps back up. One visit restores about 25%. Visit twice a week and it never drops below full. The primary repair mechanism is the thing the whole app is for.

Paying restores it — for convenience

Away for a fortnight, or holding land across town? Spend Stride instead: about 12% of what the plot earns. It is a real cost, but a small one, and only ever paid by people who choose convenience.

The effect is that active players almost never pay upkeep, and people holding land they never visit pay it constantly. That is exactly the right way round.

Ground Rent — replacing the Great Burn

What we removed

"At the end of every season, the top 100 landowners burn 10% of everything their land earned." A surprise charge aimed at the most successful players, destroying value they had already banked.

What replaces it

A small, published, predictable Ground Rent on every plot — and it is waived entirely if you visited the plot at least six times during the season.

LevelEarns per seasonGround RentAs a share of earningsIf you visit 6× a season
12,7001505.6%nothing

Six visits in ninety days is one visit a fortnight. Anyone who genuinely uses their land will never pay a penny of rent.

Why this is the better mechanism

  • It is predictable. You can see next season's rent today.
  • It taxes holding, not earning. A burn punishes the players who did best. A rent charges the players who are doing nothing. That is the difference between a fine and a bill.
  • It is avoidable by playing. The way out of the charge is to go for a walk.
  • Everyone already understands it. Council tax, ground rent, service charge. No explanation needed, no outrage.
  • It frees up dead land. Rent unpaid for two seasons returns the plot to the map.
Where the idea comes from

This is a land value tax — an idea from the economist Henry George, and one of the very few taxes economists broadly agree is efficient. It charges people for occupying scarce ground rather than for the work they do on it. That is precisely the behaviour we want: hold land you use, release land you do not.

Part 3 · The token

Seasons that shrink, then settle

You asked to keep the 90-day season and the falling reward pool. Both stay. What changes is that the fall stops at a floor instead of heading towards zero.

The arithmetic problem in version 2

Pools shrinking 20% every season add up to about 45 million tokens ever issued. But the plan reserved 600 million for player rewards. The two numbers were thirteen times apart, and any analyst would have found it in ten minutes.

The fix

The pool falls quickly for five years, then levels off at 2.5 million per season. The 600 million reserve now funds rewards for fifty-six years, so curve and allocation describe the same economy.

Fast decline while the network is small and rewards should be generous; a long, flat, dependable tail after that.

Why a floor rather than a slow fade to nothing

  • An economy needs a heartbeat. A reward that approaches zero eventually means the season means nothing, and seasons are the rhythm of the whole game.
  • New players must still have a reason to arrive in year eight. A permanent floor means joining late is worse than joining early, but never pointless.
  • It matches how currencies actually work. Bitcoin halves to a hard stop; most national economies target a small, steady, positive rate instead. We are choosing the second, because we want a working economy rather than a collectible.
  • And nothing depends on it anyway. Because everyday play is paid for in Stride, a smaller $MOVE pool never makes the game unaffordable — it only makes $MOVE rarer.

Three things the Gate achieves

No launch windfall

Early players cannot split a huge pool between a handful of accounts. Issuance always reflects real work done.

Early still beats late

Below the ceiling everyone earns the full rate. Joining early is genuinely better — without anyone being paid for nothing.

Growth cannot cause inflation

Ten times the players never means ten times the tokens. Above the ceiling, growth makes $MOVE rarer, not more plentiful.

And a rule for who qualifies at all

Only Deed holders share the season pool. Free players and Plot holders earn Stride, XP, standing, and territory — everything they need to play and progress — but not the tradeable token. This is the load-bearing rule that makes mass free signup safe: an account that costs nothing to create earns nothing that can be sold.

The route up is real, though

A free player reaches a Deed by holding a Plot for sixty days and converting it. No money required. So the gate is not a wall between paying and non-paying users — it is a wall between committed and disposable accounts, and the only currency that crosses it is time spent in a real place.

Part 3 · The token

Take 60% now, or 100% in thirty days

You asked that landowners be able to sell most of what they earn. They can. But everything arriving on the market on the same afternoon would hurt every one of them, so we let players choose their own timing — and reward patience.

A choice, not a rule. Nobody is prevented from selling, and nobody is penalised for waiting.

Why players accept this

  • Nothing is confiscated. The full amount is always available; the only question is when. Anyone who waits a month receives every token they earned.
  • It is a choice they make, not a rule imposed on them. That distinction matters enormously to how a mechanic feels.
  • It is familiar. Cashing out early for less is how notice accounts, early-redemption bonds, and pension drawdowns work. Nobody finds it strange or unfair.
  • The 40% is not destroyed. It goes back into the next season's pool, so impatient players are funding the rewards of the ones who stay. That is a story players actually like.

Part 4 · Playing together

Districts, and the empty map

A city is not a pile of plots. Seven plots make a district, districts make a city, and the ground nobody has touched yet is worth more than it looks.

Districts — the reason to concentrate

Hold a majority of a district's plots and every one of them earns more. It is the difference between owning scattered squares and owning a neighbourhood.

Plots you hold in a districtAll your plots there earnWhat it means

Remember that the Ground Rent rate rises with concentration too. Holding a whole district is a genuine achievement with a genuine cost, not a purchase.

The Frontier — why empty ground is valuable

Most of the world has never been walked by a MovenRun player. That empty ground is not dead space; it is the cheapest opportunity in the game, and we pay people to go and find it.

First-footing bonus

The first player ever to move through a cell earns 3× Effort for that session, and the plot is permanently marked with their name as its discoverer.

Frontier plots are cheap

Quiet ground costs about seven days of walking. A new player in an untouched town can build a district faster than a veteran fighting over a city centre.

It spreads the game outward

Without it, everyone would crowd the same downtown cells and the map would feel small. Rewarding exploration keeps a city interesting and keeps new towns viable.

It solves the cold start

The first fifty players in a new city are the ones with the most to gain — which is precisely the incentive a network needs at the moment it has the fewest people.

The balance we are aiming for

A city centre is expensive, contested, and lucrative. A quiet suburb is cheap, safe, and modest. The Frontier is free, uncontested, and rewards discovery. Three genuinely different ways to play, and no single correct one — which is what stops a territory game collapsing into everyone fighting over the same three streets.

Clubs and shared treasuries

Clubs are the cheapest way we will ever acquire players, and the strongest reason anyone stays. They need a real economy of their own, not a chat room with a badge.

Why clubs matter economically

They are our acquisition channel

Running clubs, walking groups and gyms already exist and already meet. One captain brings thirty players in one conversation — and thirty players in one neighbourhood is instant density.

They multiply retention

Leaving a game is easy. Letting down a team you see every week is not. Social ties are the single strongest retention mechanism in any product of this kind.

They are a large, willing sink

Members contribute readily because the benefit is visible and shared. A club of thirty routinely spends far more than thirty individuals ever would.

Guarding against fake clubs

A club of thirty accounts run by one person would be an obvious way to farm shared rewards. Three rules make it pointless:

  • Rewards scale with distinct verified movers, not member count. Thirty accounts moving on one phone count as one.
  • Diminishing returns per member. The tenth member adds meaningfully less than the second; the fiftieth adds almost nothing. Big clubs win by being active, not by being large.
  • Club rewards are paid in Stride and standing, never in $MOVE. There is nothing sellable to farm.
Part 4 · Playing together

How a contest actually runs

"City wars" needed to stop being a phrase and become a set of rules anyone could referee. Here they are.

The shape of a season

How a district is won

District score = the total Effort your club's members recorded inside that district this week, counted once per person per day, multiplied by the share of plots your club holds there.

Three things follow from that one sentence, and each is deliberate:

  • You must actually go there. Effort counts only inside the district's boundary. No amount of running elsewhere helps.
  • Numbers matter, but people matter more. Counting once per person per day means a club of twenty ordinary walkers beats three athletes running loops all day.
  • Owning land is an advantage, not a victory. Holding plots multiplies your score but cannot win on its own — a club that stops showing up loses the district it owns.

Three of those four create no new supply. Contests move value between players rather than printing it — which is why they can run weekly without inflating anything.

How sponsorship actually works

This is our best revenue line, so it needs to be a product a shop owner can understand in one minute and judge in one month.

A pub spending $300 a month buys roughly 400 verified people past its door — about 75p each, with a report to prove it. A newspaper advert cannot be measured at all.

Why a sponsor should trust the numbers

Every visit is verified

The checks that guard the token guard the footfall report. A sponsor buys verified movement, not impressions.

Counted once per person per day

Walking past six times counts once. It keeps the number honest and stops players farming a sponsor's budget.

Minimum footfall to qualify

We will not sell sponsorship on a plot too quiet to deliver. Better to decline than hand over zeroes.

Measured against a baseline

Every report shows the four weeks before the campaign beside it. If it did not work, they see that too.

The rule that protects the game

A sponsor can add rewards. A sponsor can never take anything away, gate anything, or change who wins a contest. Sponsored plots are ordinary plots with a bonus attached — players who ignore them are never worse off. The moment sponsorship starts distorting the game, players stop trusting the map, and the audience we are selling stops existing.

Part 5 · Safety

Twelve ways to cheat, and why each fails

Written as an attacker would write it. If a defence here is weak, it is better that we find it than that someone else does.

The attackHow it would workWhy it does not pay
1 · Fake the GPSSpoof a route from the sofaSpeed, accuracy, continuity and duration checks; each route usable once; a signature required before anything is created
2 · Drive the routeCover 40 km in a carAnything above ~80 km/h is rejected — and beyond 10 km the reward is zero anyway
3 · Farm by distanceRun all day, every dayThe Effort curve flattens at 10 km. A marathon earns 4.7× a short walk, not 56×
4 · Mass free accountsThousands of accounts walkingFree accounts earn only Stride, which has no market price and cannot leave the app
5 · Farm then upgradeBuild Stride on free accounts, convert to $MOVEStride can never convert to $MOVE. There is no exchange rate to exploit
6 · One phone, many accountsRun several accounts on one deviceDevice attestation; one Home Plot per verified person; a plot's value counts distinct movers, and a device cluster counts once
7 · Friendly challengesAttack a friend's plot to farm rewardsPrizes come from the loser's stake. It is zero-sum between them; no new tokens exist to farm
8 · Wash-trade landSell deeds to yourself to fake a marketA 2.5% fee each way, loyalty resets to zero on transfer, and Ground Rent keeps accruing. Round-tripping costs real money and destroys the land's defensive value
9 · Corner a districtBuy every plot in a valuable areaGround Rent rises progressively with concentration — triple rate beyond sixteen plots. And you must still visit them all
10 · Bot clubsThirty accounts, one operatorClub rewards count distinct verified movers with diminishing returns per member, and pay only in Stride and standing
11 · Milk a sponsorWalk past a sponsored shop fifty timesCounted once per person per day. Fifty visits pay exactly as much as one
12 · Buy a whole city earlyArrive with capital, take the mapYou cannot claim a plot without visiting it five times in a fortnight. Capital cannot substitute for being there
The principle underneath all twelve

Anything that can be automated earns nothing sellable, and anything sellable requires being in a real place over real time. Every defence above is an application of that one rule. It is also why the free tier can be completely open — an account that costs nothing to make is worth nothing to farm.

Stated honestly

No location system is perfectly spoof-proof, and we do not claim otherwise. A determined attacker with real devices moving in real places can extract some value — but they would be, in effect, playing the game. The design goal is not perfection; it is that cheating costs more than playing.

Part 5 · Safety

Borrowed from real economies

Almost nothing in this design is new. Each mechanism is something countries or long-running games have already tested at scale — which is the main reason to believe it will work.

Our mechanismWhere it comes fromWhat it solves
Days of WalkingIndex-linked pensions and wagesStops a shrinking reward quietly making everything unaffordable. The number changes; the effort never does
Ground RentLand value tax — Henry GeorgeCharges people for occupying scarce ground rather than for the work they do on it. Speculators pay; residents do not
Progressive rent bandsIncome tax bandsMakes cornering a district expensive without punishing ordinary players
Vault limitClash of Clans storage capsTurns a growing balance into circulating money by giving everyone a standing reason to spend
Two currenciesEvery successful game economy since the 1990sKeeps everyday play affordable while something genuinely scarce still exists
Cost and income rising togetherClash of Clans upgrade curvesThe grind never lengthens, so players never hit a wall and quit
Quick Claim discountNotice accounts, early-redemption bondsSpreads selling out without confiscating anything from anyone
Emission floorCentral bank inflation targetsAn economy needs a small steady heartbeat, not a fade to zero
Growth GateProduction quotasGrowth cannot inflate supply; it can only make each share smaller
Revenue ValveSovereign wealth funds — NorwayWhat can be paid out is bounded by what the enterprise actually earns
First-footing bonusHomestead actsRewards the people who go where nobody has been, which is where a network most needs them
Dormancy reclaimAdverse possessionLand that nobody uses returns to circulation instead of freezing the map
The thread running through all of it

Every one of these mechanisms does the same job in a different place: it keeps value moving. The failure mode of a game economy is not too little money — it is money sitting still, in balances nobody spends and land nobody uses. Almost every rule in this document exists to keep something circulating.

What these three show

The person who spends nothing still owns land, joins a club, and reaches a deed. The person who pays gets there faster and deeper, but is a net spender of tokens. And the person trying to extract value finds the cheapest route is to become an ordinary player. All three outcomes are the ones we want.

What this needs, and what is still open

This is a design, not a shipped system. Here is honestly what has to be built and what we have not settled.

What has to be built

PieceWhere it livesNotes
StrideBackend onlyNever on the blockchain. Earning, Vault, spending, upkeep. The largest single piece of work, and the cheapest to run.
Effort curveBackendSits in the existing verification service. A small change to code that already works.
Plots & levelsBackendPlots are off-chain until they become Deeds. Cheap, fast, no fees.
Days of Walking indexBackend, read on-chainA rolling 30-day median, published for anyone to check.
Season pool & Growth GateNew contractThe deployed contract pays a fixed rate per kilometre. This replaces it.
Quick Claim / Full ClaimNew contractStraightforward vesting logic; well-understood patterns exist.
Deeds with escrowNew contractFixes the deployed bug where a defender could refuse to hand over land they had lost.
Ground RentNew contractReplaces the Great Burn, which currently does not burn anything anyway.

Four new contracts. That was already necessary — our own audit lists fifteen defects in the deployed set, two of them critical. This design does not create the need for new contracts; it decides what they should say.

What we have not settled

Open questionWhy it matters
Is 100 Stride per Effort right?It sets the scale of every price in the game. Easy to change before launch, painful after.
Is 38 days the right payback?Shorter feels generous but empties sinks; longer feels like a grind. This needs real players to settle.
Will 4% of players convert a deed each month?The single assumption the whole sink model rests on. Untested.
$9 for a deed — right price?Low enough to be impulsive, high enough to stop mass sybil accounts. Both ends need testing.
Six visits a season to waive rentGenerous enough that nobody is caught out, strict enough to free dead land. A guess for now.
Does Quick Claim get used?We assume half. If almost nobody uses it, sell pressure is later but larger.
Region rules for tradeable rewardsNeeds legal advice we have not yet paid for. Free play is unaffected either way.
The honest summary

The structure of this economy is sound and borrows from mechanisms proven at national and game scale. The numbers are calibrated but unvalidated — every one of them comes from our own modelling rather than from watching real players. The first city exists to replace roughly a dozen assumptions with measurements, and we should expect several of them to be wrong.

The nine sentences

The three questions people will ask first

"Won't it get too expensive to keep up?"
No — prices are set in days of walking, so they move with earnings, and every upgrade pays for itself in about five weeks whatever level you are on.
"How does a new player with no money get land?"
Their first plot is free on day one. The next one costs about a fortnight of walking and five visits — no money, no token, no wallet at any point.

Free accounts earn Stride, which has no market price and can never convert into $MOVE. The only way to the tradeable token is holding real land in a real place for sixty days.
