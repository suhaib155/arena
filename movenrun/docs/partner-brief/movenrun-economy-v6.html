Section 1

The decision

Five versions produced good pieces and one bad habit — each rewrote everything. V6 takes the best part of each and stops.

FromWhat we keepWhat we drop
V2Locked MOVE for free players — one token, two states, never convertsThe Great Burn; the assumption that land prints income
V3Front-loaded effort curve; prices indexed to earningsStride — a second currency that hollowed out token demand
V4Private base camp; non-exclusive residency; honest burn accountingCoverage rule that collapses; land expiring every season; too many gates
V5Many recurring sinks; the reflexivity testComplexity; fourteen systems nobody could hold in their head
The single change that makes it work

V3 invented a second currency so free players had something to spend. That was the mistake: it meant free play created no token demand. V6 pays free players in the real token, permanently locked. They can spend it on everything in the game, and every token they spend is destroyed — from the same capped supply the buyers hold. Free players stop being a cost and become the engine that shrinks supply.

What each kind of player gets

Free player — locked for ever

  • Earns real $MOVE by moving
  • Spends it on gear, land, defence, style
  • Can never sell, send or convert it
  • No wallet, no purchase, no crypto knowledge
  • Plays the whole game, competes, wins

On-chain buyer — the rights layer

  • Holds liquid $MOVE they bought or earned
  • Votes on parameters and treasury
  • Stakes for standing and priority
  • Owns tradable deeds and can sell them
  • Trades on the marketplace

Both spend the same token on the same things. The difference is not power inside the game — it is ownership rights outside it. A free player is never beaten by a wallet; they are only ever beaten by someone who moved more.

Section 2

Why this burns more than it prints

This is the mechanism the whole design rests on. It is worth understanding precisely, because it is what no previous run-to-earn game had.

Locked tokens can only be spent inside the game.
Everything spent inside the game is destroyed.
So every locked token ever minted eventually leaves the supply for good.
Of six million tokens released, nearly five million are destroyed and one million can ever reach a market.

The property that makes growth safe

Read that table again

The more free players there are, the more deflationary the economy becomes. In every previous run-to-earn game, growth meant more tokens hitting the market and was the thing that eventually killed them. Here, growth means more tokens locked into a form that can only be burned. Mass adoption is now the safest thing that can happen to us, not the most dangerous.

Section 4

Six ways to spend it

The burn engine. Every one recurs, every one is something players want, and every token spent is destroyed.

1 · Gear

Craft boots, jackets and packs from materials found in different terrain. Parks give one thing, hills another, night walks another. Looks and capability — never extra earnings.

2 · Recharge

Gear and land hold a charge that drains as you use them. Walking through your own land recharges it free; paying recharges it instantly. The most frequent spend in the game.

3 · Upgrade

Land levels up. Each level costs more and does more — more quest slots, stronger defence, more style options. Never more income.

4 · Defend

When a rival challenges your land you can fortify it, up to three times. Costly, temporary, and the difference between holding and losing.

5 · Protect

Insure land before a contest and recover part of your outlay if you lose. Removes the fear that stops people competing at all.

6 · Style

Colours, map themes, banners, titles, share cards. Pure sink, no advantage, and the easiest thing in the world to sell to someone enjoying themselves.

The calibration that matters

A typical player earns about 60 tokens a season and finds about 67 worth of things they want. The gap of seven is bought — roughly 20 cents a month at a realistic token price. Small enough that nobody feels pushed, large enough that across a city it is the demand that absorbs everything the sellers sell.

Section 5

Supply that can never run away

This is where StepN and Axie died. Their supply grew with their player count. Ours cannot.

The trap they fell into, named plainly

In Axie, breeding a creature was the biggest way to spend — and it produced a new creature that minted its own tokens. The main sink was really an investment in more inflation. Nothing in our six sinks creates a new earner. Gear wears out, charge drains, fortifications expire, insurance pays out and ends. Everything is consumed.

The three rights, and what each is for

Voting

Holders vote on emission settings, sink prices, season rules and treasury spending. Snapshot-based, timelocked, published in advance. The economy belongs to the people holding it, not to us.

Staking

Lock tokens for a season to gain standing, priority in land contests, and a larger voting weight. It pays no interest — it buys position, not yield. That is deliberate: interest just makes piles bigger.

Deeds

Land held long enough can be minted as a tradable deed with its full history. Sellable, rentable, sponsorable — the one genuinely scarce asset in the game.

The bridge between the two

A free player who wants the rights layer has a route: hold land for sixty days, then mint it as a deed. That mint has a token cost, payable in cash or in tokens bought on the market. Nothing about being free is permanent except the locked state of tokens already earned — and those stay locked for ever, which is exactly what stops farm-and-dump.

Why this feels like Web3 rather than a company account

Every deed, every vote and every stake is on Base, under the holder's own keys. We cannot revoke a deed, reverse a vote or freeze a stake. What we control is the game; what the holders control is the economy's settings and their own property. A free player is never controlled by us either — they simply hold a balance that was minted non-transferable and always will be.

Section 7

Land, and nobody locked out

Two people can live on the same street. Only one can run the zone. Separating those two ideas removes the collision entirely.

LayerHow manyWhat it is
HomeOne each, freeYour own base. Private, never shown to anyone, cannot be taken. A hundred players in one street each have one.
ResidencyUnlimitedLocal membership, earned by five visits in a fortnight. Everyone who walks there can hold it at once. It blocks nobody.
CharterOne per zoneThe right to run the zone — name it, style it, set quests, host sponsors. Earned by contribution, renewed automatically while you stay active.
The incentive that makes it pleasant

A charter holder's standing rises with how busy their zone is. So they want newcomers there. A new player is not a threat to be resented — they are what makes the zone worth holding. That is the opposite of every virtual land project so far.

Zones start large. When one reaches around forty regular residents it splits into seven at the next season boundary; the holder keeps one, six open up. Land supply follows people, never a schedule.

What this means for cost and risk

Eleven of the twelve systems this economy needs already exist as working screens with tested logic. The economy is not a rewrite — it is a ledger, four contracts, and connecting what is already there. That is the single strongest argument that this design is buildable rather than aspirational.

The four contracts

Token with two statesMint locked or liquid from one capped supply; locked can never transfer

These replace the deployed test contracts, which our own audit already found to carry fifteen defects, two of them critical. The redeploy was necessary regardless; this design decides what the new contracts should say.

Notice the released column never changes. That is the point — the pool is fixed by schedule, not by how many people show up.

Reading the shock case

In a downturn, players stop buying deeds, so the free share falls and more of the pool is minted liquid. Burn falls, selling rises, and the ratio drops from 4.8× to 3.3×. It gets worse — and it stays positive. More is still destroyed than sold, because locked tokens keep burning whatever the price does. That is the property no previous run-to-earn game had: our burn does not depend on confidence.

What would actually break this

RiskWhat happens, and what we would do
Players do not spendThe biggest risk by far. If people hoard locked tokens instead of spending them, burn stalls. Mitigated by charge draining, gear wearing out and seasonal deadlines — but it needs proving with real players.
Nobody buys the top-upThe seven-token gap goes unfilled and sinks shrink. Survivable — the economy just runs smaller — but it removes the demand that absorbs selling.
Too few deed holdersFewer people to sell, which is fine, but also fewer buying deeds and voting. Governance stays thin.
Sinks feel like a taxIf recharge is resented rather than enjoyed, players stop rather than pay. Wear rates must be tuned generously and tested early.
Locked balance grows unspentAdd an expiry — unspent locked tokens lapse after 180 days and are destroyed. Only introduce this if hoarding actually appears.
The honest summary

The structure is sound and fails safely: supply is capped, the pool never grows with the player base, no sink creates a new earner, and the majority of every season's release can only ever be burned. The calibration is entirely ours. Roughly eight numbers here — participation rates, costs, the free/paid split — are estimates. The first city replaces them with measurements, and several will be wrong.

Section 10

The whole thing, once

Eight sentences

  1. Everyone earns the real token by moving — the first kilometre counts most, so a short daily walk is a genuine way to play.
  2. Free players' tokens are locked for ever: fully spendable inside the game, never sellable, never convertible.
  3. Buyers get the rights — voting, staking, tradable deeds and the marketplace — but no advantage inside the game.
  4. Six things to spend on: gear, recharge, upgrade, defend, protect, style. All recurring, all consumed.

  6. The pool is a fixed schedule from a capped supply — twice the players never means twice the tokens.

The three questions you will be asked

"If free players can't sell, is the reward real?"
Completely. It buys everything in the game — gear, land, upgrades, defence, style. What it cannot do is leave. That is what protects the token for the people who did buy in, and it is stated plainly at signup.
"Why won't this end like StepN?"
Their supply grew with their player count and their main sink created new earners. Ours is a fixed schedule, and nothing you can buy makes more tokens. When their players lost confidence, minting carried on; when ours do, locked tokens keep burning anyway.
"What stops someone farming free accounts?"
Free accounts earn only locked tokens, which have no market value and never convert. There is nothing to farm. Selling requires a deed, which requires holding real land in a real place for sixty days.
