Part A · Section 01

What a token actually is

Forget everything you have heard about crypto for two minutes. A token is a much simpler idea than the noise around it suggests.

Start with a shared notebook

Imagine a notebook in a village square. Anyone can write in it, everyone can read it, and once something is written it can never be erased — you can only add a new line. Thousands of people keep identical copies, so if one person forges their copy, every other copy disagrees and the forgery is rejected.

That notebook is a blockchain. It is not a currency or a company. It is just a way of keeping a record that nobody controls.

A token is a number in that notebook

If the notebook can record who owns what, it can record quantities too. A token is simply a balance written in it: "address #4821 holds 350 units." That is the whole idea. What makes tokens interesting is not the technology — it is the rules attached to them, which are also written into the notebook and cannot be quietly changed later.

The rules are the product

How many can ever exist, who can create new ones, what they can be used for, and what destroys them. These rules are the tokenomics.

Nobody can cheat them

The rules run as a program on the blockchain. Not even the company that wrote them can print extra tokens or change the cap afterwards.

That is why it is used at all

A company database can be edited overnight. A public ledger cannot. That credibility is the only reason to bother with any of this.

NFTs became famous through cartoon profile pictures, which is an unhelpful association. The underlying idea is just "this specific unique thing belongs to this specific person, recorded where nobody can revoke it." Land registries and certificates are the natural use — and ours is literally a land registry.

The sentence to remember

A token is a balance in a ledger nobody owns, governed by rules nobody can secretly change. Everything else in Part A is about who gets to create those balances, and why anyone would want one.

Part A · Section 02

Where tokens come from

Somebody has to create the first token. How they are created is the single biggest fork in the road between a project that lasts and one that does not.

Four ways new tokens come into existence. Only the fourth has a mechanism for taking them back out.

Model 3 in detail, because it is the one we had to fix

"Emitted for activity" sounds ideal: users do something useful, the protocol rewards them, everyone wins. It fails for a reason that is obvious in hindsight.

If the only way tokens enter is printing, and the only thing a user can do with them is sell, then supply rises forever while demand depends entirely on new buyers arriving. The moment new buyers slow down, the price falls; the moment the price falls, the reward is worth less; the moment the reward is worth less, users leave — which slows new buyers further. It is a spiral with no floor.

What MovenRun does differently — three changes

  1. Tokens are only created for proven movement. There is no faucet, no airdrop, no "connect wallet and claim". A cryptographic proof that someone genuinely moved is required before a single token exists. See Section 14.
  2. The supply that can be created is capped and shrinking. Every season issues from a fixed pool, and each pool is deliberately smaller than the last. See Section 10.
  3. Most tokens are destroyed by playing the game. Claiming land, fortifying it, challenging a rival — all of it consumes tokens permanently. See Section 12. This is the piece the previous generation simply did not have.
The design rule we followed

Decide how tokens leave the system before deciding how they enter it. That order is unusual, and it is the reason our economy has a reason to exist beyond new buyers arriving.

Part A · Section 03

How a token gets a price

This is the part people find most mysterious, and the part that matters least to our business. Both facts are worth understanding.

A token has no price until someone trades it

A token sitting in a ledger has no value, the way a concert ticket nobody wants has no value. A price only exists once there is a market — a place where someone will exchange the token for something else, usually a stable currency.

In crypto that place is usually an automated pool: the team (or anyone) deposits, say, 100,000 tokens alongside $100,000 of stable currency. The pool then quotes a price from the ratio between the two sides, and it moves as people buy or sell. Buying takes tokens out and pushes the price up; selling puts tokens in and pushes it down.

A token price is only ever the balance between these two columns. Our design work is almost entirely about the left column.

Why our business does not depend on this

Here is the crucial commercial point. MovenRun's revenue is not the token price. When a café pays to sponsor a zone, that is a normal invoice in normal currency. When someone subscribes to premium tools, that is a normal subscription. Neither transaction cares what $MOVE trades at.

The token price matters to us in exactly two ways: it affects how rewarding the game feels to crypto-native players, and a healthy economy makes land more desirable. Both are real, and neither is our income statement.

Say this precisely

$MOVE does not trade anywhere today. There has been no sale, no listing, no liquidity pool, and no allocation to anyone. It exists only as a test-network contract with no value. Never describe it as an investment, never quote or estimate a price, and never imply anyone will earn income from it. That is both the honest position and the legally safe one.

Part A · Section 04

Why crypto economies collapse

Four failure patterns account for nearly all of it. Every design decision in Part B is a direct answer to one of them, so it is worth knowing them by name.

1The printing spiral

What happens: tokens are printed as rewards, users immediately sell them, supply grows faster than demand, price falls, rewards become worthless, users leave.

Our answer: a fixed season pool that shrinks each season, so total issuance is bounded and declining rather than proportional to how many people show up (§10). Plus sinks that destroy far more than a typical game ever collects back (§12).

2Rewards with nowhere to go

What happens: the token has no genuine use inside the product, so the only rational action on receiving one is to sell it. Every reward becomes immediate sell pressure.

Our answer: you cannot claim land, fortify it, challenge a rival, run a club, or enter a season without spending $MOVE. The most committed players are net consumers of the token, not net sellers (§12).

3Free-farm-to-dump

What happens: anyone can sign up, farm rewards at zero cost, sell them, and repeat with a new account. The economy funds its own attackers.

Our answer: two reward states (§8). Free players earn Locked MOVE, which can be spent inside the app but can never be transferred or sold. Only active Zone Deed holders — people who have already spent to acquire and defend land — can earn sellable rewards, and even then only from a capped pool. Crucially, upgrading does not unlock old rewards: locked stays locked forever.

4The company that was only a token

What happens: the project's entire income is token sales and token appreciation. When the market turns, there is no revenue, no runway, and no reason for the team to stay.

Our answer: the whole of Part C. Sponsors, land fees, subscriptions, and events are paid in ordinary money by people who want something other than a token.

Part A · Section 05

What "digital land" really means

The phrase sounds like science fiction. The mechanism is closer to a parking permit than to buying a field.

The map is divided into permanent cells

We lay a honeycomb over the entire planet using an open standard called H3, originally built by Uber. Every point on Earth belongs to exactly one hexagon with a permanent identifier. Our cells are about 0.74 km² each From our code — roughly a small neighbourhood. Two people standing on the same corner are always in the same zone, anywhere in the world.

A Zone Deed is a record that a specific cell is yours

When someone claims a cell, an entry is written to the public ledger: "hexagon #8828308281 belongs to address #4821." That entry is the Zone Deed. It is not a picture of a place — for the game's purposes, the deed is the place. It carries who holds it now, the history of how it changed hands, how strongly it is defended, and what it has earned.

What it is not — be precise about this

A Zone Deed is a game asset, not real-world property. It confers no rights over the physical land, no access, no planning permission, and no relationship with whoever actually owns the ground. Anyone who implies otherwise is misrepresenting the product.

Why scarcity is real here, and not manufactured

Most digital scarcity is arbitrary — a team simply declares that only 10,000 items will exist. Ours is bounded by geography and by effort:

There is only one of each place

The hexagon containing the main square of your city exists exactly once, for ever. No amount of demand creates a second one. That is genuine, non-arbitrary scarcity.

Land must be earned, not just bought

A cell only becomes claimable once at least 5 distinct people have genuinely moved through it over 90 days From our code. One person cannot manufacture a valuable district alone.

Busy land costs more

The claim price scales with how active the cell is — 500 × √(weekly movers) From our code. A quiet suburb is cheap; a busy high street is expensive, automatically.

Holding it costs effort

Rivals can challenge your land, and a cell left untouched for 180 days can be flagged and eventually reclaimed From our code. Land is a commitment, not a collectible.

Why this matters commercially

Scarcity that comes from geography plus verified effort is far harder to copy than scarcity that comes from a press release. A competitor can clone our app in a quarter; they cannot clone the fact that the people in your neighbourhood already hold the ground around your street.

Part B · Section 08

One token, two states

This is the most important mechanism we have, and the clearest answer to "how is this not another move-to-earn?". Users feel one simple economy; underneath, the protocol keeps engagement and market liquidity apart.

The same token, held in two different states. Which state you earn in depends on whether you have skin in the game.

The rule that makes it work

The no-retroactive-unlock rule

When a free player later becomes a landowner, their existing Locked balance stays locked for ever. Only rewards earned after they qualify can be Liquid. This single rule closes the loophole that would otherwise sink the whole design: farm for months on free accounts, upgrade once, and cash out everything at once.

Why this is fair rather than a paywall

It would be easy to read this as "free players get the worthless version". It is the opposite, for three reasons:

  • Locked MOVE buys real progress. Everything that makes the game deeper — capturing, upgrading, fortifying, running a club, entering events — is purchasable with it. A free player is never blocked from the fun.
  • Landowners pay first. Nobody earns Liquid rewards without having already spent to acquire land and continuing to spend to defend it. They are net contributors to the economy before they are beneficiaries of it.
  • Free players create the value. Density is what makes land worth owning and what makes sponsorship worth buying. Free players are not a cost centre — they are the reason the economy exists at all.

Part B · Section 10

Where new tokens come from

Not a fixed rate per kilometre for ever. Rewards come from a capped seasonal pool that every active player shares — and the pool shrinks every season on purpose.

The formula, in words

Your reward = the season pool × (your activity score ÷ everyone's activity score)

More players means more competition for the same pool. Fewer players means the active ones earn more.

This one change fixes the deepest flaw in the old move-to-earn model. Under a fixed rate per kilometre, ten times as many users automatically means ten times as many tokens printed — growth itself destroys the economy. Under a pool, growth cannot inflate supply. It only changes how the same fixed amount is divided.

The emission schedule Planned — the deployed contract does not yet do this

Four 90-day seasons make one year. Total year-one issuance: 26.6 million $MOVE — just 2.7% of the maximum supply.

Why a shrinking pool is the right shape

  • Early players are rewarded more, and should be. They take the most risk and do the hardest work — populating an empty map.
  • Supply cannot run away. Even unlimited user growth cannot print an extra token.
  • It forces the economy to mature. As emissions fall, the value of holding land, earning land tax, and real sponsor money has to take over. That transition is the whole business plan, and the schedule makes it non-optional.
Two honest gaps you should know about before anyone else finds them

1. The deployed contract does not do this yet. Our live test contract mints a fixed 10 $MOVE per kilometre with a halving every six months — the older design. Moving to seasonal pools requires a new contract version. It is on the plan and it is not yet built.

2. The schedule and the distribution do not currently reconcile. Shrinking 20% per season converges to about 45 million $MOVE issued in total, ever — but our stated distribution reserves 600 million (60%) for player rewards. Those two numbers are 13× apart. Either the decay must flatten to a sustaining level after the first years, or the reward allocation is far larger than the emission plan needs. This must be resolved before any public tokenomics is published. Section 30 lists it as an open item.

How to present this

Finding your own inconsistency and naming it is a strength, not a weakness. The wrong move is to publish two numbers that a diligent analyst can pull apart in ten minutes. The right move is: "here is our emission curve, here is our allocation, we know they do not yet reconcile, and here are the two ways we can close it."

Part B · Section 11

The four safety valves

A capped pool sets the ceiling. Four automatic valves sit underneath it, each able to reduce rewards further when the economy is not healthy. None of them can increase rewards.

Four independent brakes. Any one of them tightening reduces issuance; none of them can loosen it.

The one that investors care about most

Valve 2 — the revenue valve — is the differentiator worth leading with. It says: the amount of sellable reward the protocol issues is bounded by the real revenue the business generates. If sponsorship and subscription income grow, the reward pool can be supported. If they do not, rewards tighten automatically rather than being propped up by new buyers.

That single rule turns the token from a promise into a claim on demonstrated business performance, and it means the honest answer to "what backs $MOVE?" is "a real revenue line, and a cap that respects it" rather than "belief".

Part B · Section 14

Only real movement earns

Every token and every deed depends on one thing being true: the movement actually happened. If someone can fake a walk from their sofa, the entire economy is worthless. This is the hardest technical problem we have.

Route plausibility Live

Speed, distance, timing, and continuity are checked. Anything implying more than ~80 km/h is rejected — which catches the simplest cheat, which is driving.

Signal quality Live

Phones report how confident they are in each position. A session where too many points are vague is rejected, because vague points are how spoofing tools hide.

One-use proofs Live

Each route is compressed into a unique fingerprint that can be claimed exactly once. You cannot record one good walk and submit it a hundred times.

Economic caps Live

Even a perfect cheat is bounded by a hard daily limit per address, and a cell needs 5 distinct movers over 90 days before it is worth anything.

Device integrity Planned

Real-device attestation and emulator resistance, so a farm of virtual phones cannot pose as a neighbourhood.

Social corroboration Planned

Cross-checking against nearby activity and route patterns. A cell where only one account ever appears is treated with suspicion.

Trust score Planned

Risk-based reward limits and a manual review queue, so unusual accounts earn less until they establish a history.

Stake-to-earn slashing Planned

Landowners have deposits at stake. Proven cheating costs them their deposit and their eligibility — cheating becomes expensive rather than free.

Say this honestly, because it is the strongest answer

No GPS system on earth is perfectly spoof-proof, and we do not claim to be. What we claim is layered defence: several independent checks a cheater must defeat simultaneously, plus economic caps that make the effort not worth the reward. Verification is treated as a permanent ongoing investment, not a solved problem. An investor who has seen other projects claim "unspoofable" will trust this answer far more than a confident one.

The legal posture that follows from all this

Free rewards are non-transferable in-app credits — closer to loyalty points than to money. Sellable rewards are capped, activity-based, and gated by eligibility, with clear user disclosures and region rules. Nothing in the product is offered as income, investment, or return. This posture is not decoration: it is what keeps a movement game a game rather than a financial product, in every market we might launch in.

Section 22 shows what happens to our model if conversion is a third of the optimistic case.

Why the "never earning power" rule is non-negotiable

The moment a subscription increases what a player earns, three bad things happen at once. The game becomes pay-to-win, which drives away the free players whose density everything depends on. The rewards become harder to describe as a game feature and easier to describe as a financial return, which is a regulatory problem. And the sponsor proposition weakens, because footfall starts reflecting who paid rather than who genuinely moves. Holding this line costs us some short-term revenue and protects the entire model.

Why our acquisition cost should be unusually low

The Base ecosystem thesis

Our first community should not be "global fitness". It should be Base itself — projects, cities, clubs, creators and sponsors already competing for attention, given a physical arena to compete in.

Gasless claims

Free players claim badges and zones without ever holding a gas token. Removes the step where most newcomers give up.

Share-card virality

Base-native share cards, leaderboard claims, and invites turn every captured district into distribution.

Why this is the right first market

Base is explicitly looking for consumer applications that bring non-crypto users on-chain — that is what its accelerator exists to fund. We are a consumer product whose entire design goal is exactly that, and whose contracts are already deployed on Base's own test network. The fit is structural, not opportunistic. And unlike a general fitness launch, the Base community is reachable, concentrated, competitive, and already holds budget for sponsorship.

  • Distribution most consumer apps cannot buy. Ecosystem promotion to an audience actively looking for apps to try.
  • A warm sponsor pipeline. Base projects sponsoring city wars are a far shorter sales cycle than cold-calling cafés — and they prove the format before we take it to local business.
  • Credibility with partners. Base is operated by a large regulated company, which matters when a council or a race organiser is deciding whether to work with us.
  • Density by default. A concentrated community launching together solves the cold-start problem that geography would otherwise make brutal.

Part D · Section 27

The questions you will be asked

Ten questions, with answers that are accurate and correctly hedged. Learn the shape of these rather than the words.

1 · How is this not another move-to-earn that collapses?
Three structural differences. The asset is territory, not a token balance, so progress is ownable rather than only sellable. Sinks were designed before incentives — you must spend to claim, fortify, and defend, so demand does not depend on new buyers. And most rewards can never be sold at all: free players earn a locked, non-transferable credit. On top of that we have an enforced internal rule blocking any tradeable economy until verification, density, and real sponsor demand are all proven.
2 · What happens to your business if the token goes to zero?
Three of our four revenue engines are unaffected — sponsorship, subscriptions, and events are paid in ordinary money by people who want footfall, tools, or a race experience. The land economy would be hurt. The company would not be existentially threatened, which is precisely the structure the last generation lacked.
3 · What stops people faking GPS?
Layered defence: a speed gate, an accuracy gate, continuity and duration checks, a route fingerprint usable exactly once, a cryptographic signature the contracts require, a hard daily cap per address, and a rule that a cell needs five distinct movers over ninety days before it is worth anything. No GPS system is perfectly spoof-proof and we do not claim to be — we claim several independent checks a cheater must beat at once, plus caps that make it not worth the effort.
4 · Why will anyone sponsor a zone?
Because they are buying verified physical footfall with a measurable report, not impressions. A café gets players choosing routes past its door and a dashboard showing how many did. Honest caveat: we have not signed one yet, and doing so is one of our top milestones.
5 · What is your actual traction?
Zero users and zero revenue. What we have is verifiable engineering: eight contracts deployed to Base's test network with 49 passing tests, a working GPS verification service, a security and identity foundation, and a mobile app with real movement tracking. We are pre-traction and precise about it.
6 · What is the biggest risk?
Density, not technology. A territory game with no neighbours is a lonely map, and reaching enough players in one city is harder than anything in our codebase. Our whole go-to-market — clubs, city-by-city launch, Base-native competition — exists to attack that one risk.
7 · What stops a bigger company copying this?
The app can be copied in a quarter. What cannot is the fact that the people in a neighbourhood already hold the ground around their streets, the sponsor relationships in that city, and a verification system that is genuinely hard to get right. The moat is geographic and social, and it only exists where we have already won.
8 · Is the token a security?
Our design is deliberately built to avoid that: free rewards are non-transferable in-app credits closer to loyalty points; sellable rewards are capped, activity-gated, and never offered as income or return; nothing is sold to the public today. We have not yet taken formal legal advice, and doing so is a funded line in our use of funds. Never assert a legal conclusion we have not paid someone to give us.
9 · Your contracts are already deployed — are they safe?
The deployed version is a testnet reference implementation, not production-ready. We catalogued sixteen defects ourselves, two of them critical, each proven by a passing test, and published the register. They require a V2 redeploy plus an external audit before real value moves. Volunteering this is the strongest available answer, because the alternative is them finding it.
10 · Why now, and why you?
Now, because embedded wallets and sponsored fees have only recently matured enough to hide crypto completely — two years ago every user would have had to buy a token before their first walk — and because fees on Base are finally low enough for a game with thousands of small daily transactions. Us, because we built the difficult half first and can point at it, and because we have shown we would rather publish our own defects than ship on top of them.
